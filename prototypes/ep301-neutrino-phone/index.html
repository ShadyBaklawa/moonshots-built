The dashed blue arc and the solid orange chord above are two routes across the same circle — the one flattened plane that passes through both cities and Earth's center. A straight line between two points on a circle is always shorter than the curve between them, so the chord (through the core) always beats the arc (over the surface). This cutaway is that one plane, not a view of the globe from above, so city A always sits at the top regardless of its real compass bearing to city B.

Thought experiment, not a product. This assumes a neutrino beam could be aimed precisely enough to punch straight through the Earth and be caught on the other side — detecting a handful of neutrinos at all requires buildings full of equipment today, let alone a steerable point-to-point link. The animation is slowed down roughly —× so the race is visible; real transit times are listed above. Distances and light-speed latency are computed exactly from the two cities' coordinates — the "real fiber" toggle's slowdown is an illustrative estimate of light in glass, not a precise measurement. As stated on the podcast, Alex Wisner-Gross described wanting a neutrino-based link that shoots straight through the Earth, beating fiber or satellite paths that must follow the surface or orbit — everything else about building this is speculation beyond what was said in the episode.
